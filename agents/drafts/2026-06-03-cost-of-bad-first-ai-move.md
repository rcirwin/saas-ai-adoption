A bad first AI move doesn't cost you the build. It costs you the three things you can't see on the invoice.

Most founders price the risk as "we waste a sprint." That's the cheap part. The real bill comes due in places that never show up in the engineering estimate.

I've watched a handful of B2B teams make a thin first move, ship a chat box or a summarizer because it was the fastest thing to demo, and then quietly pay for it three ways.

**Direction.** The first AI thing you ship becomes the thing your team defends. Roadmap, hiring, the next three quarters all bend toward it. A wrong first move isn't one wasted feature. It's a wrong heading you compound on.

**Credibility.** You only get to tell your users "we're an AI company now" once before they decide whether to believe you. Ship the obvious bolt-on and the next real thing you build inherits the skepticism. Internally it's worse. The board reads a weak first move as "they don't know what they're doing here," and that read is hard to walk back.

**Position.** While you're shipping the safe demo, the move that actually mattered, owning a workflow end to end instead of adding a button to it, is still sitting on the table. Someone takes it. The cost of the wrong move is the right one you didn't make.

None of this is an argument for moving slow. It's an argument for knowing which move you're making before you make it. A first move you can defend in one sentence is cheap. One you picked because it demoed well is the expensive one.

If your AI roadmap got deleted tomorrow, which single bet would you rebuild first, and could you say why in one line?
