Your product was designed for a user who is about to stop showing up.

Not because they churned. Because they sent an agent in their place.

For 20 years we built B2B software around a human sitting at a screen, reading the dashboard, clicking through the flow, holding the context in their head. Every design decision assumed that person. The onboarding, the empty states, the tooltips, the careful UI.

An agent needs none of it. It doesn't read your dashboard, it reads your data. It doesn't follow your flow, it calls your endpoints. It doesn't get nudged by your tooltip, it gets blocked by your missing API.

So three things change the moment the agent becomes the primary user.

**Interface.** The screen stops being where value gets delivered. It becomes a place humans go to supervise. The real surface is your data and your actions, exposed cleanly enough that something non-human can complete the job without a guided tour.

**Judgment.** A human user brought their own judgment to your tool. The agent doesn't. Whatever the agent needs to know about your domain has to live in the product now, encoded, or the agent improvises and gets it wrong. The model is the engine. The harness is the product.

**Loyalty.** A human picks a tool and sticks with it through the rough edges. An agent re-evaluates every call. If the slice you own is 1 of 7 steps, the agent routes around you the moment something completes more of the job.

Glean didn't win enterprise search by adding a chat box. It won by being the thing an agent can query and trust across every system.

The shift isn't "add an agent to your product." It's deciding whether your product survives once the agent is the user reading it.

If your product can only be used by a human, who is your real buyer once the human delegates the work?
